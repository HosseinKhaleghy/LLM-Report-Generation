# Dairy-Farm Energy Knowledge Base

Reference guidance for the RAG-grounded advisory report. Each `##` section is
one retrievable chunk. Figures are typical industry values for advisory use,
not farm-specific guarantees. Expand or correct sections freely — the retriever
re-embeds the file automatically.

## Plate cooler / milk pre-cooling
A plate cooler (pre-cooler) uses mains or well water to drop warm milk from
~35 C towards ~15-18 C before it reaches the bulk tank. It can remove roughly
50-60% of the heat the refrigeration system would otherwise handle, cutting
bulk-tank cooling electricity by a similar share. Pre-cooling is usually the
single most cost-effective efficiency measure on a dairy farm and typically
pays back within 1-2 years. Effectiveness depends on a water-to-milk flow ratio
of about 2:1 to 3:1.

## Heat recovery from the refrigeration compressor
A heat-recovery unit captures waste heat rejected by the milk-cooling
compressor and uses it to pre-heat water. It can supply roughly 50-60% of a
dairy's hot-water demand, directly reducing the electricity the water heater
must provide. Heat recovery pairs well with milk cooling because the cooling
load and the hot-water demand both peak around milking.

## Variable-speed drive on the vacuum pump
The milking vacuum pump often runs at full speed continuously during milking
even though full airflow is only needed intermittently. A variable-speed drive
(VSD) matches pump speed to actual vacuum demand and can cut vacuum-pump
electricity by around 50-60%. The vacuum pump is one of the largest motor loads
in the parlour, so a VSD is a high-impact upgrade.

## Water heating: scheduling and off-peak tariffs
Water heating for udder preparation, plant washing and bulk-tank cleaning is a
major dairy load. Heating water on a timer so the cycle finishes just before
milking — and shifting it onto a night/off-peak electricity tariff — reduces
cost without changing total energy. Avoid keeping large volumes hot for many
idle hours.

## Water heater insulation and temperature
Hot-water cylinders and the associated pipework lose heat continuously; a
poorly insulated tank is a common source of high overnight baseline load. Fit
an insulation jacket and lag the pipes, and set the thermostat no higher than
the wash temperature actually required (around 75-85 C for circulation
cleaning). Lowering an over-set thermostat and insulating the tank cut standing
losses immediately.

## Parlour and shed lighting
Lighting that is left on continuously contributes to baseline load. Switching
parlour and shed lighting to LED reduces lighting energy by 50-70% and the
lamps last far longer. Occupancy sensors and timeclocks prevent lights running
in empty buildings overnight. Long-day lighting regimes for the herd should be
on a controlled schedule, not left fully on.

## Ventilation and cooling fans
Shed ventilation fans and cow-cooling fans run hardest in warm weather and add
to summer consumption. Thermostatic or variable-speed fan control runs fans
only as fast and as long as conditions require. Good natural ventilation
reduces reliance on powered fans.

## Benchmark: baseline / phantom load
On an efficient dairy farm the baseline (idle) load — power drawn when no
milking or washing is happening — is typically only about 10-15% of the average
hourly demand. A baseline share well above this points to always-on equipment:
an uninsulated or over-set water heater, refrigeration short-cycling, a
continuously running pump, or lighting left on. The idle load runs 8760 hours a
year, so reducing it yields large annual savings.

## Benchmark: electricity use per cow
Dairy parlour electricity use is often in the order of 300-500 kWh per cow per
year, depending on milking system, milk cooling, water heating and whether
efficiency measures are fitted. Consumption per cow well above the local fleet
median suggests scope for milk pre-cooling, heat recovery or vacuum-pump VSD
improvements.

## Benchmark: where dairy electricity is used
In a typical dairy parlour, milk cooling and water heating together account for
roughly 60% of electricity use, the milking machine (vacuum and milk pumps)
around 20-25%, and lighting plus other loads the remainder. Efficiency effort
is best directed first at milk cooling and water heating.

## Pattern: milking-time load peaks
Dairy electricity demand is strongly shaped by milking. Sharp peaks appear in
the morning and again in the late afternoon or evening, driven by the vacuum
pump, milk pump, milk cooling and water heating running together. Between
milkings the load should fall back close to the baseline; peaks that do not
subside indicate equipment running outside the milking routine.

## Pattern: seasonal consumption
Dairy energy use shifts with the season. Cold months raise water-heating demand
because incoming water is colder and udder-prep and washing need more heat.
Warm months raise milk-cooling and ventilation demand. A farm with seasonal
(spring) calving also sees consumption rise and fall with the number of cows
being milked.

## Fault signature: water heater stuck on
A water heater whose contactor or thermostat has failed can draw power
continuously instead of cycling. The signature is a sustained rise in the
overnight or between-milking baseline, often by several kW, lasting hours or
days. It is a frequent cause of an unexpectedly high baseline share and is
worth checking first when idle load is elevated.

## Fault signature: refrigeration short-cycling
A bulk-tank refrigeration unit that switches on and off rapidly (short-cycling)
— from low refrigerant charge, a dirty condenser, or a faulty thermostat —
wastes energy and stresses the compressor. The signature is repeated brief
high-consumption spikes outside milking. A condenser clean and a refrigerant
and controls check usually resolve it.

## Fault signature: outage or tripped breaker
A sudden drop of consumption to near zero, or far below the expected level for
that time of day, can indicate a tripped breaker, a blown fuse or a supply
interruption. If it coincides with milking time it may mean equipment failed to
start; staff would normally notice, so a flagged low-consumption period is
worth confirming against the farm log.

## Fault signature: vacuum or milk pump problem
A milking pump drawing more power than usual can indicate worn components,
incorrect vacuum settings, air leaks forcing the pump to work harder, or a
failing motor. The signature is elevated consumption during milking windows
specifically. Servicing the pump and checking for leaks restores efficiency.

## Bulk tank and plant washing loads
Automatic washing of the milking plant and the bulk tank uses both hot water
and pump energy, and runs after each milking and after collection. Efficient
wash routines, correct water volumes and temperatures, and not over-heating
wash water keep this load in check.

## Solar PV and renewable generation
Because dairy electricity demand has strong daytime peaks around milking and
washing, on-farm solar PV can offset a useful share of consumption, especially
where milking and water heating can be aligned with daylight. PV is most
attractive after the cheaper efficiency measures (pre-cooling, heat recovery,
VSD, insulation) have been taken.

## Monitoring and sub-metering
Sub-metering the main loads — milk cooling, water heating, the milking machine
— makes it possible to see which equipment drives consumption and to catch
faults early. Continuous monitoring turns a once-a-year bill into an ongoing
management tool and verifies that efficiency measures are delivering.

## Off-peak tariff scheduling
Where a time-of-use electricity tariff is available, shifting flexible loads —
water heating, bulk-tank washing, and any pre-cooling water pumping — into the
cheaper night or off-peak window reduces cost even when total energy is
unchanged. Timers and simple controls make this straightforward.

## Benchmark: load factor
A dairy farm's load factor (mean hourly consumption divided by peak hourly
consumption) typically falls between 0.55 and 0.75 on a well-managed unit.
A load factor below 0.5 indicates a large gap between average and peak demand,
which may signal sharp, uncontrolled peaks during milking or washing that could
be staggered or smoothed with timers and controls.

## Benchmark: baseline share of mean hourly consumption
On an efficient dairy farm the baseline (idle) load is typically 10–15% of
mean hourly consumption. A baseline share above 25% of the mean indicates
a large always-on load — commonly an uninsulated or over-set water heater,
a refrigeration unit short-cycling overnight, a continuously running pump,
or shed lighting left on. Note: the baseline share is measured against the
mean, not the peak; the two yield very different percentages and must not be
confused.

## Benchmark: per-cow annual consumption ranges
Typical dairy-farm annual electricity consumption:
- Efficient farms (plate cooler, heat recovery, VSD): 250–330 kWh per cow per year
- Average Irish / Northern European farm: 300–450 kWh per cow per year
- Inefficient farms (no pre-cooling, poor insulation): 450–600 kWh per cow per year
Farms above 450 kWh/cow/year have clear scope to improve milk cooling and
water-heating efficiency. Farms approaching 600 kWh/cow/year should treat
energy reduction as a priority before considering renewables.

## Fault signature: consecutive high-consumption anomalies
When several flagged high-consumption hours occur on the same day or in the
same narrow window, the likely cause is a single piece of equipment running
continuously at elevated load: a water heater whose thermostat has failed open,
a refrigeration compressor locked on, or a motor running against a blockage.
Consecutive anomalies on a single day are more likely a sustained fault than
independent events and should be investigated as a single incident.

## Fault signature: seasonal anomaly clustering
If anomalous hours cluster in particular months or seasons — more highs in
winter, more lows in summer — the pattern points to a seasonal cause rather
than a random equipment fault. Winter clusters of high consumption often
indicate a water heater struggling in cold weather; summer low-consumption
clusters can indicate cooling equipment cycling off prematurely due to a low
refrigerant charge or dirty condenser.

## Bulk tank sizing and efficiency
An oversized bulk tank runs its refrigeration unit less efficiently because
the compressor cycles against a large thermal mass. An undersized tank
overworks its compressor. The correct bulk tank capacity for a given herd
size is approximately 2.5–3 days' milk yield. Refrigeration efficiency also
depends on condenser cleanliness: a dirty condenser raises head pressure and
can increase cooling electricity by 10–15%.

## Water heating load and proportion
Water heating typically accounts for 25–35% of total dairy electricity use.
The main demands are udder preparation (warm water), milking-plant circulation
cleaning (hot water at ~75–85°C), and bulk-tank washing. Reducing water
heating energy starts with insulating the hot-water cylinder, fitting a timer
to heat only ahead of milking, using a heat-recovery unit fed by the bulk-tank
compressor, and checking that the wash programme is not heating more water
than the cleaning cycle needs.

## Milking machine efficiency: vacuum system
The vacuum system (vacuum pump, pulsators, liners) can account for 15–25% of
parlour electricity. Common efficiency losses include worn liners causing
air leaks that force the pump to run harder, incorrect vacuum levels set too
high, and pumps running for long periods between milkings when they could be
stopped. An annual service covering liner replacement, vacuum level check and
pump inspection maintains efficiency and prevents elevated milking-window
consumption.

## Sub-metering and smart monitoring
Fitting separate energy meters on the bulk tank, water heater and milking
machine allows each load to be tracked independently. This turns an anomaly
flag into a direct identification: if the bulk tank sub-meter shows a spike
on the same hour as the anomaly, the fault is localised. Smart monitors that
send alerts when a load exceeds a threshold catch faults within hours rather
than at the next bill. The investment in sub-metering typically pays back
within one year through faster fault detection.
